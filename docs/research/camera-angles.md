# Camera Angles and Zoom in 2D Side-View Games

**For:** Small Nukem — a single-file HTML5 2D side-view plant-startup game (WebGL 2, phone portrait primary). The player (Fluke) walks decks, climbs ladders, opens control panels and operates them; a follow-the-Chief mode exists where Fluke trails the Chief to a panel for a guided demonstration. Current camera (**SN_ZOOM**, shipped): the camera looks **ahead of the player toward the target panel**, zooms in smoothly on approach up to **1.8×**, eases back out; fit guarantees hold at every zoom (pop-ups never cover the player; touch targets ≥ 2 cm at any zoom). Art is painterly/photoreal renders, **not** pixel art.
**Question:** how do other 2D side-view games handle camera framing ("angles") and zoom — what are the proven techniques, what numbers are actually published, and what should Small Nukem keep or change?

*All sources below were read via the web index on 2026-11-26. Provenance is flagged per claim because the field publishes unevenly: **primary** = developer talks/postmortems and engine documentation; **frame-counted** = a third party measuring shipped games from footage (explicitly approximate); **unofficial spec** = fan reverse-engineering documents; **review evidence** = critic observation, used only where no developer source exists. Where no number is published for a named game, this report says so rather than inventing one.*

## Summary

1. **The field's default is not "camera centred on the player."** It is a layered rig: a **dead zone** (camera window) the player moves inside without moving the camera, **lookahead** that shifts the frame toward where the player is going or looking, and **smoothing** (damping) between the camera and its target. Itay Keren's GDC talk — the canonical survey — catalogues ~20 named variants of this rig across forty years of side-scrollers, and every modern example in this report is some combination of them (https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/scroll-back-the-theory-and-practice-of-cameras-in-side-scrollers, `index`).
2. **Small Nukem's core idea is a recognised, named technique — and a good fit.** Framing the *interaction point* rather than the player is Keren's **target-focus**; framing *two* subjects by averaging and zooming to fit is **position-averaging + zoom-to-fit** (Super Smash Bros., Never Alone, Pyre). The shipped design is inside the mainstream, not an experiment.
3. **The numbers converge.** Frame-counted measurements of shipped games put dead zones at **~20–35% of screen width**, lookahead at **~8–15% of screen width**, and damping at **~6–20 frames** depending on the game's pace (https://www.rozgames.com/article/camera-framing-2d-platformers-dead-zones-lookaheads, `index`). Engine rigs express the same ideas in time: Phaser's published follow-rig uses a **0.4 s lookahead** and eases zoom at the same rate as position; Cinemachine exposes lookahead in **seconds** and warns that noisy targets make it jitter (URLs in §1.5, §2.4).
4. **Zoom is used sparingly and on context changes, not continuously.** The shipped examples zoom to *fit a set-piece* (Smash Bros., Yoshi's Story, Disney Illusion Island), to *emphasise an interaction* (Dave the Diver's catch zoom), or as *cinematics* (Metroid Dread). No source found recommends tying zoom continuously to movement in a slow game — and Keren's comfort research (§2.6) explains why: conflicting visual motion is the classic nausea trigger. Small Nukem's approach-triggered zoom is the right shape; a speed-linked zoom would not be.
5. **The vertical axis is where cameras go wrong, and ladders are the known hard case.** The proven rules: don't chase jumps (track up only on landing — platform-snapping), treat the axes unequally, and when the player is on a rope/ladder/climb, switch to free vertical follow (Donkey Kong Country: Tropical Freeze does exactly this). Small Nukem's ladders are precisely the case the exception exists for (§1.4).
6. **Pixel-art zoom constraints do not apply to Small Nukem.** Integer scaling exists to protect a texel grid; painterly/photoreal art has none, so continuous fractional zoom (1.0–1.8×) is safe. What *does* carry over: keep the HUD in screen space at fixed size across all zoom levels (§2.5).

The concrete, builder-ready rules are in §5.

## Findings

### 1. Framing and "angles": the follow models

A 2D side-view game has no camera angle in the 3D sense; "angle" is **framing** — where the view sits relative to the player, what it includes, and how it moves. Keren's GDC 2015 talk "Scroll Back: The Theory and Practice of Cameras in Side-Scrollers" is the field's reference taxonomy, and his framing is worth adopting wholesale: a camera must serve **Attention** (show what matters), **Interaction** (support what the player is doing), and **Comfort** (never fight the player's vestibular sense — conflicting visual vs. balance signals cause discomfort and nausea, and this is "still very much in effect in 2D games") (https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/scroll-back-the-theory-and-practice-of-cameras-in-side-scrollers, `index`).

#### 1.1 The basic models

- **Position-locking (hard follow).** Camera glued to the player (Rally-X, Kung-Fu Master). Keren notes it works in Terraria's case because the character is small relative to the screen and jumps are small — the conditions under which hard follow stops being nauseating. It is the model almost every later game softens.
- **Camera-window / dead zone.** The player moves freely inside an invisible rectangle; only when they push its edge does the camera move (first seen in Jump Bug's last level). **Rastan Saga** made its window **as tall as a standard jump**, so jumps never move the camera vertically — at the cost of seeing less above, and of uncomfortable leftward movement in a narrow window. **Fez** keeps a horizontal window through its dimension shifts and uses smoothed position-locking vertically. **Shinobi** pairs a very wide vertical window with a continuous slow snap back toward the ninja.
- **Platform-snapping.** The camera holds its vertical position during a jump and **snaps to a new baseline when the player lands** (Super Mario World; Sonic uses it to maintain vertical alignment on platforms). SMW adds **dual anchors**: two resting camera heights with a threshold the player must cross before the anchor switches, so frequent up/down hops don't pump the camera.
- **Lerp-smoothing / damping.** The camera eases toward its target each frame instead of arriving instantly (Donkey Kong Country was an early user). The stronger form is **physics-smoothing** — a critically damped spring (Unity's SmoothDamp): Keren's summary is that "if Lerp is EaseOut, physics-smoothing is EaseInOut" — it accounts for the camera's existing velocity, so it starts gently, can overrun slightly, and settles without a jerk (Never Alone).
- **One-way vs bidirectional.** The original Super Mario Bros. window is **one-sided** — no backtracking — with a "virtual speedup point" ~25% off-centre so the camera is already moving at speed when Mario reaches the edge (no 0→full-speed jump in one frame). Bidirectional games need the lookahead machinery of §1.2 instead; Keren's catalogue and the GMTK episode both treat the two cases separately (https://usecaddie.app/s/how-to-make-a-good-2d-camera, `index` — transcript of https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TdWFzpgnljs).

#### 1.2 Lookahead: framing where the player is going

A centred character wastes half the screen on where they've been. Every serious rig shifts the frame forward; the variants differ in *what* drives the shift:

- **Facing/direction lead, eased.** Cave Story keeps two anchor positions and **glides** between them on direction change — the switch speed scales with walking speed (base snapping speed + walk speed), so the lead never teleports. New Super Mario Bros. U biases the frame right of Mario and only swings left after *sustained* backtracking. Hyper Light Drifter offsets ahead of facing. Bonanza Bros. uses dual-forward-focus and catches up to a new target at ~2× player speed, moving only when the player moves (all per Keren, `index` above; GMTK covers the same cases, `index` above).
- **Velocity/projected lead.** Defender (1981) framed an imaginary point **~25% of screen width ahead** of the ship. Secrets of Rætikon extrapolates the focus from current velocity (projected-focus); Keren warns it extrapolates badly on the vertical axis — jumps and landings — which is one more reason the axes get different rules (§1.4).
- **Speed-scaled lead.** Sonic Generations grows the lead with speed up to a maximum and settles back onto Sonic at rest (GMTK, `index`). Sonic's window is narrow horizontally (he needs the ahead view at speed) and taller vertically (small jumps shouldn't move the camera) (Keren, `index`).
- **Input/target lead.** Jazz Jackrabbit 2 takes focus from controller input on both axes and recentres on release; Snapshot averages player and pointer; **The Swapper focuses the cloning device's target spot** — the direct ancestor of Small Nukem's "frame the panel, not the player" (§1.3).

Frame-counted measurements (third-party, explicitly approximate) put shipped lookahead at **~15% of screen width** in Super Mario World (shifting over ~10 frames, snapping back on stop/reverse), **~12%** in Celeste, and **~8–10%** in Hollow Knight; the same source's tuning guidance is to start at **10% of screen width** (https://www.rozgames.com/article/camera-framing-2d-platformers-dead-zones-lookaheads, `index`).

#### 1.3 Focus beyond the player: target-focus, cue-focus, regions

The techniques Small Nukem's camera actually belongs to:

- **Target-focus** — the frame is driven by what the player is interacting with or aiming at, not the avatar (The Swapper; Luftrausers combines target-, projected- and cue-focus). SN_ZOOM's "look ahead toward the target panel" is target-focus with the panel as the target.
- **Cue-focus** — invisible attractor/repeller points placed by designers pull the frame toward things worth seeing (Limbo puts attractors on enemies; Luftrausers). This is the level-design-friendly version: a cue on each panel would generalise SN_ZOOM's single-target logic.
- **Region-focus / region-based anchors** — the level is divided into regions, each with its own camera behaviour: Limbo sets per-region framing and zoom for ambience; Donkey Kong Country lets designers set per-region behaviour (runner regions get a wide forward view, exploratory regions a wider view both ways); Keren's own Mushroom 11 uses **dozens of regions, each with a dedicated camera path and a preset zoom (FOV factor) per region**. Vessel is the instructive case for a panel-operating game: inside a region the camera is **almost stationary but still follows the player with minor movements** (preserving the player's sense of control), and it is **already in motion when leaving a region**, which minimises the jarring acceleration into the next framing.
- **Position-averaging (multi-focal)** — with two subjects, frame their average: Gauntlet and Samurai Gunn average player positions; Never Alone averages its two characters with weights. This is the base model for follow-the-Chief framing, combined with zoom-to-fit (§2.1).

#### 1.4 The vertical axis: jumps, falls, ladders

The consistent finding across Keren, GMTK and the frame-counted measurements: **treat the axes unequally, and do not chase vertical movement.**

- **Ignore jumps; re-baseline on landing.** A camera pinned to the player moves on every jump — "nausea + hard to line up jumps" (GMTK). Donkey Kong Country: Tropical Freeze ignores jumps and tracks up only when the player lands on a sufficiently high platform (a new baseline); Rayman Legends tracks up only in predetermined areas; Super Mario World and Rayman snap gently on landing (platform-snapping, §1.1).
- **Design the vertical problem away where possible.** Shovel Knight's levels are built so their **maximum height equals the exact screen height — the camera never moves vertically at all**; single-screen rooms remove camera movement entirely for precision sections (GMTK, `index`). Its levels are literally constructed screen by screen — "There are 26 screens for every level, give or take a screen or two", following Mega Man's 25-screen formula, with the screen transitions "entirely Mega Man" (https://www.prosyscom.tech/gaming-news/how-shovel-knights-developers-modernised-nes-game-design-gaming-news/, `index`; https://gameverse.com/2013/05/03/interview-the-crew-at-yacht-club-games-talks-shovel-knight-part-i/, `index`). A reviewer noted the payoff: discrete screens make "each screen a discrete spatial puzzle" (http://www.eurogamer.net/articles/2014-06-26-shovel-knight-review, `index`).
- **The climb exception.** DKC: Tropical Freeze's no-vertical-chase rule has one stated exception: **when climbing a rope (or swimming), the camera moves freely** (GMTK, `index`). Keren's catalogue has the same idea as **gesture-focus**: Tomba shifts the camera when the player climbs or moves between depths, including shifting behind the player for a better view ahead. A ladder climb is a sustained, deliberate vertical traverse — the opposite of a jump — and both sources treat it as its own mode.
- **Falls.** Offset the hero toward the **bottom** of the screen for more headroom in the direction of travel (GMTK), and note Metroid's older solution: the character may leave the window span and the camera **speeds up to catch up**, with overshoot depending on entry speed (Keren).

#### 1.5 Room-based vs free-scroll, and measured rigs

- **Room-based.** Celeste snaps its camera to room boundaries and **pans smoothly over 15 frames (0.25 s at 60 fps)** between rooms (unofficial fan spec — flagged as such: dead zone 16×16 px in its 320×180 internal resolution, per-frame lerp `camera += (target − camera) * 0.15`, look-ahead 8 px in the movement direction; https://github.com/jcbjcbjc/gamegen-verifier/blob/HEAD/spec/celeste.md, `index`). Note the tension with the frame-counted figure of a ~35%-width dead zone for Celeste (rozgames, `index`): the spec's small window is in game pixels at low internal resolution; both are reported here with their provenance, and neither is a developer-published number.
- **Free-scroll with regions** (§1.3) is the middle path most modern games take — and the one that fits Small Nukem's three open areas.
- **Engine rigs publish the canonical parameter set.** Cinemachine's Framing Transposer (designed for 2D/orthographic cameras) exposes: dead zone width/height ("do not move the camera when the target is within this range"), a soft zone beyond it (camera eases to reframe, rate set by per-axis **Damping**), **Lookahead Time in seconds** ("the point where the target will be this many seconds into the future"), lookahead smoothing, an option to **ignore Y in lookahead**, and screen X/Y placement of the target. Its documented warning transfers directly: lookahead "is sensitive to noisy animation and can amplify the noise, resulting in undesirable camera jitter" (https://docs.unity.cn/Packages/com.unity.cinemachine@2.8/manual/CinemachineBodyFramingTransposer.html, `index`). Phaser's published follow-rig gives one concrete shipped-style configuration: deadzone 160×90, smoothing 0.08 (fraction of the gap closed per step, **zoom eases at the same rate**), screen offset (0, −40) — more sky above the hero — and **lookAhead 0.4 (seconds of the target's motion)** (https://phaser.io/packs/rig-follow, `index`).
- **A secondary reference tuning** (a published camera-follow skill doc, not shipped-game data — labelled as such): at a 128×128 viewport, 60 Hz — smooth rate 2.6/s; dead zone ±22 px X / ±18 px Y; look-ahead lead 32 px eased at rate 3.4/s (≈31 px after 1 s of walking, ≈1 px after 1 s at rest — i.e. the lead itself is damped, never flipped in one frame); room transitions 0.55 s smoothstep, snapping immediately on spawn (https://www.claudepluginhub.com/skills/mulosbron-game-dev-skills/camera-follow-2d, `index`).
- **Smoothing must be frame-rate independent.** A fixed per-frame lerp fraction is the most common camera bug: the same 0.1 fraction gives ~90 px of lag at 30 fps and ~19 px at 144 fps. The correct forms are exponential decay `1 − exp(−rate·dt)` (63.2% of the way after 1/rate seconds at any frame rate) or a critically damped spring stepped with its exact solution — a naively stepped spring overshoots above ~7.1 Hz stiffness at 60 Hz. In one published lab comparison the spring held lag within 0.88 tiles where lerp lagged up to 2.92 tiles (https://nomicon.app/gameplay/camera-and-juice/, `index`). A camera-systems reference restates the working range: rates ~5–12 for exponential/SmoothDamp-style smoothing (`index`, same source family).

#### 1.6 Named games where no developer numbers exist — stated plainly

- **Dead Cells (Motion Twin):** no published numeric camera spec was found. Its official update notes do establish two facts: the game uses camera zoom in gameplay (an early-access changelog lists "Improved performance of camera zoom"), and a later update **added options to customise the camera's behaviour — the influence of the player's movement, combat, or points of interest can be adjusted** — i.e. target-focus/cue weighting exposed to the player as sliders; another note records "Camera follow speed slightly increased" from a community suggestion (https://tuxdb.com/game/8532&gamenews=1&bdate=1695647249, `index`).
- **Hollow Knight:** no developer-published camera spec found; the frame-counted figures in §1.2/§4 (dead zone ~20% width / ~25% height, lookahead ~8–10%, damping ~15–20 frames — the heaviest of the three measured games, and deliberately outrunnable in fast traversal) are third-party approximations, and its aggressive **cinematic framing** (boss-arena establishing pans, zone reveals) is the documented-by-observation part (rozgames, `index`).
- **Ori and the Blind Forest (Moon Studios):** no developer-published camera technique found in the sources read. Review evidence is used instead in §3, where it is directly on point.

### 2. Zoom

Keren's definition covers the whole space: **zoom-to-fit** means to "change zoom or move forward/back to provide a close-up view of relevant elements" — shipped games do both (2.5D games like Klonoa literally dolly a 3D camera along a path that provides positioning, tilting *and* zooming) (Keren, `index`).

#### 2.1 Zoom-to-fit multiple subjects

- **Super Smash Bros.** is the canonical case: the camera looks at the **average position** of the fighters and **zooms out as much as needed as the distance between them grows** (Keren). The pattern generalises: frame = bounding box of subjects + padding, zoom = whatever fits the box, clamped.
- **Pyre** zooms and moves to keep the character **and the ball** prominent — zoom-to-fit with two unlike subjects (GMTK, `index`).
- **Yoshi's Story** frames Yoshi + boss + a pillar and dollies out to fit them; **Klonoa** zooms out to show a boss, which "telegraphs what to do" — the zoom-out itself is information (Keren).
- **Disney Illusion Island** zooms out and settles on a **predetermined position** to frame a whole tricky section, then returns to tracking on exit (GMTK) — zoom-to-fit a *place*, not a subject.
- **Phaser's rig** states the boundary condition explicitly: with `follow([p1, p2])` the camera frames the middle and zooms out as the targets part, with fit padding 64 and min/max zoom clamps (0.5 / 1 in the published example) — and **zoom-to-fit acts only with two or more live targets; with one target, the zoom is the game's** (https://phaser.io/packs/rig-follow, `index`). That sentence is the cleanest statement of the two zoom regimes Small Nukem needs: one subject → the game's contextual zoom; two subjects (Fluke + Chief) → fit.

#### 2.2 Speed-based zoom

Sonic Generations scales its **lead** with speed (GMTK); the zoom analogue — pulling out as speed rises so the player sees further ahead — is the same instinct applied to scale. No source read for this report publishes a speed→zoom curve or range for a shipped side-scroller, and the technique belongs to speed games. Small Nukem is a slow, deliberate operator game: there is no speed signal worth zooming on, and §2.6 gives the comfort argument against inventing one.

#### 2.3 Focus zoom on an interaction point — what Small Nukem does

- **Dave the Diver** zooms in briefly on a catch, then returns — a short, purposeful focus zoom tied to an interaction event, cited by GMTK under camera "juice" alongside screen shake and hit-stop (GMTK, `index`).
- **Vessel's region camera** (§1.3) is the sustained version: while the player works inside a region, the camera stays near-still with only minor follow — the framing *holds* for the duration of the work and is already moving when the player leaves.
- **Mushroom 11's per-region preset zoom** (Keren) is the design-time version: the zoom level is a property of the *place*, set by the designer, not computed from the player moment to moment.
- SN_ZOOM's approach zoom sits in this family: zoom is a function of **context** (approaching/operating a panel), not of motion. Every source in this section supports that shape; none supports zoom as a continuous function of proximity alone with no state change — the distinction matters in §2.6.

#### 2.4 Cinematic vs gameplay zoom; easing

- **Metroid Dread** (2.5D, MercurySteam) separates the two cleanly in review evidence: general play runs a relatively zoomed-out camera (the higher resolution vs. Samus Returns "allows for a more zoomed-out camera"), while **cinematic** moves pull out for isolation/scale and push in for tension/claustrophobia, and melee-counter sequences change camera angle and zoom outright (over-the-shoulder for the Omega Cannon) (https://www.gamepur.com/reviews/metroid-dread-review, `index`; https://www.nintendolife.com/features/metroid-dread-has-plenty-of-other-ms-dna-and-thats-no-bad-thing, `index`; https://www.hardcoregaming101.net/metroid-dread/amp/, `index`). Small Nukem already makes the same separation — its cinematics are produced videos; the gameplay zoom should stay in its own, quieter register.
- **Alien Hominid / Castle Crashers** suspend gameplay on cinematic camera paths to frame a boss or story beat — Keren's "cut-less cutscene" (Keren, `index`). Hollow Knight's establishing pans are the same move (§1.6).
- **Easing:** no shipped game located for this report publishes its zoom easing curve or duration — **that number is not published, and this report does not invent one.** What is published: Phaser's rig eases zoom **at the same rate as position** (smoothing 0.08 per step), which is the one concrete, citable rule — zoom and position share one smoothing, so the frame never scales faster than it settles (https://phaser.io/packs/rig-follow, `index`). The smoothing forms themselves (exponential decay / critically damped spring, frame-rate independent) are in §1.5. Squirrel Eiserloh's GDC 2016 camera-juice talk supplies the related shake model — trauma 0–1 decaying ~1/s, shake = trauma², offsets read from smooth noise rather than per-frame random — for any future shake work, and GMTK adds the accessibility corollary: offer an option to turn screen shake off (https://github.com/aryx/ocaml-elm-playground/blob/HEAD/docs/claude_notes/tutorials/notes_juice.md, `index`; GMTK, `index`).

#### 2.5 Min/max ranges, HUD readability, and the pixel-art question

- **Published zoom ranges are rare.** The concrete ones found: Phaser's example fit clamps (min 0.5×, max 1× — for a zoom-*out*-to-fit rig); Mushroom 11's per-region FOV factors (values not published); Small Nukem's own 1.8× max focus zoom is at the strong end of anything described in these sources, and **no shipped side-scroller located publishes a focus-zoom maximum to compare it against — stated plainly rather than filled in.** What the sources do support: a focus zoom should be the *exception state* with a clear entry and exit (Dave the Diver, Vessel), which 1.8× satisfies if it is entered on approach and exited on leaving, and held steady while operating (§5).
- **HUD across zoom:** the universal practice in every rig documented here is that HUD/UI lives in **screen space** — Cinemachine's transposer moves only the world camera; Phaser's fit/zoom likewise affects the world view. Small Nukem already renders its HUD and pop-ups in screen space with fit guarantees at every zoom; the research says keep exactly that, and keep the shipped ≥2 cm touch-target rule as a *screen-space* guarantee (it cannot be a world-space one, or zooming out would shrink targets).
- **Pixel-art integer scaling — and why it does not bind here.** Pixel art has a texel grid that fractional scaling destroys: at a non-integer factor some pixels render wider than others, producing shimmer and an uneven grid; "a camera zoom of 1.5 reintroduces uneven pixels; keep sprite scale and camera zoom at whole numbers" (https://blog.makko.ai/pixel-art-blurry-in-engine/, `index` — the same source lists the four blur causes: linear filtering, non-integer scale, sub-pixel positions, compression). The standard fix is integer scaling from a low reference resolution — 320×180 or 640×360 reach whole numbers (6×/3×) at 1080p — with Unity's Pixel Perfect Camera rendering at the largest integer scale that fits and black-barring the remainder (https://github.com/jacobelder/jakes-skills/blob/HEAD/game-development/references/pixel-art-and-retro.md, `index`; https://free-gametoolbox.com/en/articles/pixelperfectcamera.html, `index`). **Small Nukem's art is painterly/photoreal renders with no texel grid, so none of this applies:** continuous fractional zoom between 1.0× and 1.8× is safe, and quantising zoom to integer steps — the pixel-art remedy — would actively harm it (visible scale steps during the approach zoom). The one transferable caution is filtering: smooth art under continuous zoom wants proper mip/filtering to avoid shimmer of a different kind; that is a texture-setting check, not a camera rule.

#### 2.6 The comfort constraint on zoom

Keren grounds his whole taxonomy in neurology: the visual system and the vestibular system disagreeing is what produces discomfort and nausea, and it applies in 2D. Every moving-camera choice is therefore a budget: dead zones exist to *reduce* total camera motion; damping exists to remove *jerk* (sudden acceleration) from the motion that remains; GMTK's jump rule exists because a camera that bobs on every jump spends the budget on nothing. **Continuous zoom is the most expensive motion there is** — the whole frame scales, so every point on screen moves radially at once — and no source read recommends it as an always-on function of movement. Zoom on a *state change* (arrive at a panel, start an operation, enter a set-piece) spends the budget once, on information. That is the line §5 draws for SN_ZOOM.

### 3. Mobile and portrait specifics

Purpose-built portrait side-view games are rare — most portrait 2D games re-think the axis rather than rotate a landscape game — and the honest evidence base is thinner here. What exists:

- **Downwell** (Ojiro Fumoto) was designed portrait-first for phones — in the designer's own words, "something that you could play on a portrait mode on smartphones", which produced "a really vertical kind of platformer where you keep falling down" (https://www.gamereactor.eu/falling-down-the-well-with-gun-boots-in-downwell/, `index`). The lesson is structural: in portrait, the generous axis is **vertical** — Downwell spends its long axis on the direction of travel (falling) and keeps the short (horizontal) axis trivially readable. Small Nukem inverts this: its travel is horizontal (decks) and its long axis is vertical. The consequence the sources support: portrait side-view must buy horizontal view distance with **lookahead and zoom-out headroom**, because the frame cannot provide it natively — which is precisely SN_ZOOM's job, and an argument for keeping its zoom-out state genuinely wide.
- **The Ori cautionary case.** Ori and the Blind Forest's camera sits "zoomed out quite far": fine on a large TV, but in handheld play "things are obviously very small", enemies are obscured and safe landings hard to judge; a second review describes Ori getting lost as "a dot" in busy scenes, worse in chase scenes (https://www.squarexo.co.uk/ori-and-the-blind-forest-definitive-edition-switch-review/, `index`). A fixed far zoom that works on a TV fails on a small screen. For a phone-first game this argues for Small Nukem's closer default framing plus approach zoom — and against solving portrait's narrow width by simply zooming far out.
- **Vertical FOV trade-offs.** Portrait gives vertical room the landscape games lack: the vertical rules of §1.4 get cheaper (a window "as tall as a standard jump" fits easily; multi-deck views can show the deck above/below as context), and the GMTK advice to offset the hero toward the bottom of the screen for headroom maps naturally onto portrait's tall frame. Phaser's published offset (0, −40 — more view above the hero) is the same instinct in one number (https://phaser.io/packs/rig-follow, `index`).
- **Touch safe zones.** No published portrait-platformer source located specifies touch-zone geometry — stated plainly. The applicable constraints are Small Nukem's own shipped rules, which the research does not contradict: controls and HUD in screen space at fixed size (§2.5), touch targets ≥2 cm at any zoom, and pop-ups that never cover the player. Keren's one mobile-native data point is Osmos: on tablets, **pinch-to-zoom is the intuitive manual zoom** — relevant only if a manual override is ever added (§5, R10) (Keren, `index`).
- **Test on the real display.** Both the frame-counting source and GMTK close with the same instruction: tune dead zone/lookahead/damping with the debug view visible, **on actual displays at real viewing distances** (rozgames; GMTK, `index`). For Small Nukem that means the Pixel 10's portrait screen, not a desktop window.

### 5. Recommendations for Small Nukem

Each rule is stated so a builder can implement it directly. "Keep" rules protect what the research validates; "Change/Add" rules close gaps the research exposes. SN_ZOOM's current internals were not re-measured for this report — where a rule says "verify", the first step is to check whether the shipped code already does it.

**R1 — KEEP target-focus on the panel; generalise it to one cue per panel.**
SN_ZOOM's look-ahead toward the target panel is Keren's target-focus (The Swapper) — a named, proven model, and the right one for a game whose verb is "operate that panel". Generalise from "the current step's panel" to **a cue-focus attractor on every panel** (Limbo's attractors; Mushroom 11's per-region cues), so framing help exists during free movement and Level 2 rounds, not only on the guided path. Sources: Keren (https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/scroll-back-the-theory-and-practice-of-cameras-in-side-scrollers).

**R2 — ADD/VERIFY a real dead zone: 20–35% of view width, ~25% of view height.**
If SN_ZOOM eases the camera continuously toward its target with no window, small player movements move the whole world — the "screen feels jittery" failure mode. The shipped-game band is 20–35% of screen width (SMW ~25%, Hollow Knight ~20%, Celeste ~35% frame-counted; Celeste's unofficial spec disagrees at low internal resolution — the percentage band is the safer import) and ~25% of height. Inside the zone the camera does not move at all. Sources: rozgames frame counts + tuning band (https://www.rozgames.com/article/camera-framing-2d-platformers-dead-zones-lookaheads); Fez window (Keren; GMTK https://usecaddie.app/s/how-to-make-a-good-2d-camera).

**R3 — Smoothing: frame-rate-independent, one rate for position and zoom.**
Replace/verify any fixed per-frame lerp fraction with exponential decay `1 − exp(−rate·dt)` or a critically damped spring; start at the ~10-frame feel (the frame-counted mid-point: SMW 8–12, Celeste 6–8, Hollow Knight 15–20) — in rate terms, the published working band is ~5–12/s. **Zoom eases at the same rate as position** (Phaser's rig rule) so the frame never scales faster than it settles. Guardrail from the lab data: a naive spring overshoots above ~7.1 Hz stiffness at 60 Hz — step it with the exact solution or use exponential decay. Sources: nomicon (https://nomicon.app/gameplay/camera-and-juice/); Phaser (https://phaser.io/packs/rig-follow).

**R4 — Lookahead: express it in time, cap it in screen fraction, ease every switch.**
Set the panel-ward lead as **0.25–0.4 s of travel** (Defender's ~25%-of-width point; Phaser's published 0.4 s; Cinemachine's seconds-based parameter), hard-capped at **~15% of view width** (the top of the frame-counted band: SMW ~15%, Celeste ~12%). When the target changes (new step, direction reversal), **glide** the lead — never flip it in one frame: Cave Story scales switch speed with walk speed; NSMB U waits for sustained backtracking; the secondary reference rig eases a 32 px lead at 3.4/s so it takes ~1 s to fully develop and ~1 s to fully release. Apply lookahead **horizontally only** (Cinemachine's "ignore Y" option exists for this; projected vertical lead is Keren's named failure case). Sources: Keren; Phaser; Cinemachine (https://docs.unity.cn/Packages/com.unity.cinemachine@2.8/manual/CinemachineBodyFramingTransposer.html); rozgames.

**R5 — Vertical rules: don't chase hops; re-baseline on landing; free-follow ONLY on ladders.**
(a) Small vertical movements (step-ups, hops between deck levels) do not move the camera; when Fluke lands on a new deck, snap/ease the baseline to that deck (SMW platform-snapping, dual anchors with a switch threshold so borderline hops don't pump the frame). (b) **While Fluke is on a ladder, switch to free vertical follow** — DKC: Tropical Freeze's rope exception exists for exactly this sustained-climb case, and Tomba's gesture-focus shifts framing for climbs. (c) On leaving the ladder onto a deck, re-baseline per (a). This removes the known **ladder framing fight**: a dead-zoned vertical camera and a climbing player pull against each other for the whole climb; the sources resolve it by making the climb its own mode, not by tuning the fight. Sources: GMTK (DKC: TF, Rayman Legends); Keren (SMW, Tomba).

**R6 — Zoom: state-based, held while operating, exited with the camera already moving.**
Keep the 1.8× maximum focus zoom — no source publishes a focus-zoom max to contradict it, and the Ori evidence (§3) argues against widening the *default* view on a phone. Change the trigger semantics to match the proven pattern: zoom is a function of **state** (arrived at panel / panel open / operating), entered on approach, **held dead steady while the player operates the panel** (Vessel: near-stationary region camera with minor follow — a zoom that breathes while the player reads gauges spends comfort budget and fights fine reading), and exited when the player leaves, with the camera **already in motion** as the zoom releases (Vessel's exit rule). Never tie zoom to speed or to raw proximity with no state change (§2.2, §2.6). Dave the Diver's catch zoom is the model for any *brief* zoom: event in, short hold, return. Sources: Keren (Vessel, Mushroom 11); GMTK (Dave the Diver, Disney Illusion Island).

**R7 — Follow-the-Chief: two-subject framing — average position + zoom-to-fit, clamped.**
While SHOW/follow is active, SN_ZOOM's single-target focus is the wrong model: the player needs **both** Fluke and the Chief framed (the Chief is demonstrating; Fluke must stay visible to stay oriented). Use the multi-focal pattern: frame centre = **weighted average** of the two positions (Never Alone's weighted averaging; Smash Bros.), zoom = **fit both subjects plus padding, clamped** (Phaser's fit: padding + min/max clamps; fit engages only with ≥2 live targets). Suggested clamps from the sources' shape: never zoom in past the R6 operating zoom, never out past the default wide state; if the pair separates beyond what the max zoom-out can fit, bias the average toward the Chief (he is the information source) and let Fluke ride the frame edge — Samurai Gunn shows players tolerate an uncomfortable average, but a *teaching* game should bias to the teacher. On demo end, return to R1 target-focus with the R3 smoothing — no cut. Sources: Keren (Smash Bros., Never Alone, Gauntlet); Phaser.

**R8 — Keep cinematic zoom out of the gameplay camera.**
Dread separates cinematic push-ins/pull-outs from its gameplay framing; Small Nukem already separates them physically (produced videos, full-mute rule). Do not import cinematic moves (boss-style pull-outs, counter-style angle changes) into SN_ZOOM; the gameplay camera's register stays quiet. Source: Dread reviews (§2.4).

**R9 — Add a Reduce Camera Motion setting.**
GMTK's accessibility rule (an option to disable screen shake) and Dead Cells' shipped precedent (player-facing sliders for the camera's movement/combat/points-of-interest influence) both point the same way. Minimum viable form for Small Nukem: a settings toggle that (a) caps zoom at 1.0× (approach zoom off), (b) widens the dead zone to the top of the R2 band, (c) slows smoothing to the Hollow Knight end of the band (~15–20 frames). It is also the direct mitigation for the §2.6 comfort risk for sensitive players. Sources: GMTK; Dead Cells update notes (https://tuxdb.com/game/8532&gamenews=1&bdate=1695647249).

**R10 — Do NOT add: speed-based zoom, integer-stepped zoom, or a manual zoom control (yet).**
Speed zoom: no published model fits a slow operator game (§2.2). Integer zoom steps: a pixel-art remedy that would visibly step SN_ZOOM's smooth approach (§2.5). Manual zoom: Keren notes SMW's shoulder-button camera push was "often criticized as useless", and the one intuitive manual form (Osmos pinch) would fight the panel touch controls; revisit only if play-testing shows players hunting for it. Sources: Keren; makko (https://blog.makko.ai/pixel-art-blurry-in-engine/).

**R11 — Tune on the device, with the debug view on.**
Draw the dead zone, the current lead vector, and the active framing state (R1/R5/R6/R7) as a debug overlay, and tune on the Pixel 10 in portrait at real viewing distance — the closing instruction of both the frame-counting source and GMTK. Every number in R2–R4 is a *starting point from shipped games*, not a finished value; the sources are unanimous that these parameters are tuned per game, per display. Sources: rozgames; GMTK.

#### Pitfalls in the current design, flagged per the brief

1. **Continuous-zoom comfort risk (the headline risk).** If SN_ZOOM's zoom is a continuous function of distance-to-panel, the frame scales radially for as long as the player walks — the most expensive motion in Keren's comfort model, spent on approach rather than on information. R6's state-based trigger (enter on approach, hold while operating, exit on leave) keeps the shipped *feel* while removing the continuous spend. Verify which of the two the shipped code does; the research condemns only the continuous form.
2. **Ladder framing fights.** A vertical dead zone (needed for R5a) fights a ladder climb for its whole duration. Resolved by mode, not tuning: R5b's climb exception (DKC: TF rope rule; Tomba gesture-focus).
3. **Damping eating the lookahead.** GMTK's warning: too-slow damping leaves the character *ahead* of the camera with even less effective lookahead than none; a secondary scenario measurement quantified it (a 32 px lead yielding ~16 px effective at 120 px/s under damping). R3's band (start ~10 frames, not 20) plus R4's time-based lead keeps the lead real. Small Nukem's slow walk speed makes this a minor risk, but the demo/follow mode moves faster than the player does — check it there first.
4. **Anchor-flip jitter.** If the panel target changes step-to-step, an un-eased lead flip reads as a camera jerk (Cinemachine's documented lookahead-jitter warning is the engine-side version). R4's glide rule covers it.
5. **Portrait width starvation.** Portrait's short axis is horizontal; if play-testing shows players surprised by off-screen panels, the fix order is: stronger lead (to the R4 cap) → wider zoom-out state → cue arrows — **not** a permanently farther default zoom (the Ori handheld failure: everything shrinks, nothing reads).
